How It Works: The EOQ Formula

The Economic Order Quantity formula identifies the exact point where annual ordering cost equals annual holding cost, yielding the lowest possible total cost curve.

EOQ = sqrt((2 × Annual Demand × Order Cost per order) / Holding Cost per unit per year)
  • Annual Demand (units/year): Total customer or production demand over 365 days.
  • Order Cost ($ per order): Administrative, receiving, and freight setup fee per purchase order.
  • Holding Cost ($ per unit/year): Warehousing, insurance, depreciation, and capital cost to store 1 unit for 1 year.

Worked Example (Step-by-Step)

An industrial distributor sells 12,000 valves per year. Placing each supplier purchase order costs $150, and storing one valve in the warehouse costs $4.50 per year.

Step 1: Multiply 2 by Annual Demand and Order Cost 2 × 12,000 × $150 = 3,600,000
Step 2: Divide by the annual Holding Cost per unit ($4.50) 3,600,000 / 4.50 = 800,000
Step 3: Take the square root of the result √800,000 = 894.43 units

Solved Result: Order 894 units per purchase order (placed ~13.4 times per year).

How It Works: The Reorder Point Formula

The Reorder Point formula calculates how many units your business will consume while waiting for a new shipment to arrive, then adds your safety stock cushion.

Reorder Point = (Average Daily Usage × Lead Time in Days) + Safety Stock
  • Average Daily Usage (units/day): Typical rate of daily sales or manufacturing consumption.
  • Lead Time in Days (days): Total replenishment turnaround time from order submission to warehouse receipt.
  • Safety Stock (units): Emergency reserve units maintained to prevent stockouts during variability.

Worked Example (Step-by-Step)

An electronics retailer sells an average of 45 power adapters per day. Their supplier takes 8 days to deliver restock orders, and the store maintains 120 units in safety stock.

Step 1: Calculate Lead Time Demand (Average Daily Usage × Lead Time) 45 units/day × 8 days = 360 units
Step 2: Add Safety Stock to Lead Time Demand 360 units + 120 units = 480 units

Solved Result: Trigger a new purchase order when inventory reaches 480 units.

How It Works: The Safety Stock Formula

The Max–Average Safety Stock formula measures the spread between worst-case consumption during your longest supplier delay and normal consumption during normal lead time.

Safety Stock = (Max Daily Usage × Max Lead Time) − (Average Daily Usage × Average Lead Time)
  • Max Daily Usage × Max Lead Time: Total units needed if peak daily demand coincides with maximum supplier delay.
  • Average Daily Usage × Average Lead Time: Normal expected unit consumption during standard replenishment lead time.

Worked Example (Step-by-Step)

A medical supply warehouse normally uses 45 boxes of gloves per day with an average supplier lead time of 8 days. During peak flu season, usage reaches 65 boxes/day and freight delays stretch lead time to 12 days.

Step 1: Multiply Max Daily Usage by Max Lead Time 65 units/day × 12 days = 780 units
Step 2: Multiply Average Daily Usage by Average Lead Time 45 units/day × 8 days = 360 units
Step 3: Subtract Average Lead Time Demand from Maximum Lead Time Demand 780 units − 360 units = 420 units

Solved Result: Maintain 420 units of safety stock in reserve.

How It Works: The Inventory Turnover Formula

Inventory Turnover divides the annual Cost of Goods Sold (at cost, not retail price) by the average dollar value of inventory held on the books during the year.

Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory Value
  • Cost of Goods Sold ($/year): Total direct cost of merchandise sold over the 12-month period.
  • Average Inventory Value ($): Mean inventory valuation across the period, computed as (Beginning Inventory + Ending Inventory) ÷ 2.
  • Days Sales of Inventory (DSI): 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover = average days required to convert stock into sales.

Worked Example (Step-by-Step)

An automotive parts wholesaler reports an annual Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) of $1,800,000. Their beginning inventory was $210,000 and ending inventory was $240,000, giving an Average Inventory Value of $225,000.

Step 1: Confirm Average Inventory Value ($210,000 + $240,000) / 2 = $225,000
Step 2: Divide Cost of Goods Sold by Average Inventory Value $1,800,000 / $225,000 = 8.00 turns/year
Step 3: Calculate Days Sales of Inventory (365 / Turnover) 365 days / 8.00 = 45.6 days per turn

Solved Result: Inventory turns over 8.00 times per year (every 45.6 days).
